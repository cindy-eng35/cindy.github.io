05 / DISCUSSION

From charts to OS concepts.

Technical interpretation to be supported by actual observations, not chart appearance alone.

OS interpretation
SCHEDULING & ALLOCATION

How does imbalance matter?

If some machines remain highly utilized while others are idle, resource allocation or cluster job placement may be worth examining. However, utilization traces alone cannot establish that a scheduling algorithm is inefficient.

CPU & MEMORY DEMAND

Are resource needs coupled?

CPU-heavy activity is not necessarily memory-heavy. Analyze paired observations to identify demand mismatches, while avoiding causal claims from correlation alone.

MACHINE VS. CLUSTER

What can we really observe?

Machine-level measurements summarize usage of workloads and the OS. They do not directly reveal internal kernel scheduling decisions or end-user latency.

LIMITATIONS & UNCERTAINTY

What can we not conclude?

Trace coverage is limited; sampled observations and chosen thresholds affect comparisons. High utilization does not, by itself, prove degraded application performance. Document missingness and observation windows.

EVIDENCE TO INSERT AFTER WEEK 3

Findings backed by numbers

Member D · Figures 4–5: Memory use averages 48.95% (n=187,963), and measured CPU and memory utilization show positive association across 187,962 complete pairs (Pearson r = 0.7782; Spearman ρ = 0.7844). The highest-count memory bin is 50–60%. These results describe co-occurring use, not what causes it.

Different levels of aggregation: The scatter shows 5,000 time observations for display, while the per-machine means include 1,310 machines (Pearson r = 0.8608). Pooled correlation and machine-level correlation address different comparisons and should not be treated as identical.

Member E · Figures 6–7: The heatmap shows differences in observed CPU use among machines at comparable hours. Under E's preliminary <20% / 20–<40% / ≥40% CPU categories, the high-category proportion decreases later. The last hourly interval is incomplete. The heatmap's y-axis represents sorted machine rows, not original machine IDs.

OS interpretation and limits: The traces motivate questions about placement and load balancing, but contain neither scheduling decisions nor application-level latency. The data alone cannot show wasted capacity, faulty scheduling policy or user-visible slowness. The 30/70 cutoff in D's mismatch analysis is a separate hypothesis from E's 20/40 category boundaries.

Evidence-based interpretation

  • CPU: Mean 26.47% (valid observations), with machine means from 0.68% to 47.27% among the selected examples. Machine 478 stayed almost idle; 909 and 811 varied more through time.
  • Memory: Mean 48.95%, with 63,901 observations in the 50–<60% bin. The full-sample CPU–memory Pearson correlation is 0.7782; it does not imply causation.
  • Imbalance: Member E’s hourly heatmap and category shares show different load levels across machines and times. These observations motivate further scheduling investigation but do not establish a scheduler defect.
  • Measurement limits: Neither response times, service-level performance, job placements nor scheduling decisions are present in server_usage.csv; partial last-hour coverage and potential timestamp gaps limit temporal inference.

Within-machine transitions are not scheduler transitions

Among 185,982 valid pairs of observations spaced exactly 300 seconds apart, 34,165 (18.37%) moved between Team E’s low, medium and high CPU-usage categories. This demonstrates changes in measured CPU demand. It does not reveal why demand changed, whether a job migrated, or whether the cluster scheduler behaved well or poorly. Consecutive same-machine observations and the hourly-mean heatmap answer different questions.
